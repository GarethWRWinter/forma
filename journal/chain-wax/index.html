Marginal gains 18 Feb 2026 8 min read

I have not cleaned a chain in ten years.

Not once. In that time I have ridden 360 kilometres through British rain, taken a course record, and gone under thirty minutes on Sa Calobra. The chain is the only part of your bike where the free speed is measurable, repeatable, and almost nobody buys it.

Gareth Winter

Here is the bit that annoys people. Your drivetrain is the one system on the bike where you can buy a genuine, laboratory-verified improvement for less than the price of a decent pair of bib shorts, and most riders still degrease, re-oil, and hand the difference back every single ride.

I stopped oiling chains a decade ago. What follows is why, with the numbers that convinced me, the ones that should convince you, and the honest reasons you might still decide it is not worth your Sunday evening.

The number that starts the argument

Zero Friction Cycling in Adelaide runs the most bloody-minded drivetrain testing in the sport. They measure chains on a rig, then contaminate them with a standardised grit slurry and keep measuring, out to 6,000 kilometres of simulated filth. That second part is what matters, because a chain in a laboratory is not a chain on a Sussex lane in February.

3.8W

Hot melt wax, friction loss at 250W

Against 4.5W to 7W for standard oil-based lubricants, depending on the product. Source: Zero Friction Cycling.

Call it three watts on a good day against a mediocre oil. Three watts sounds like nothing. Three watts is nothing, in isolation. That is precisely the point of marginal gains: nobody ever won anything with one of them, and nobody serious ignores them either.

Put three watts against a real effort. Sa Calobra is 9.4 kilometres at seven per cent. Hold 300 watts up it and three watts of recovered drivetrain loss is worth roughly eighteen seconds. I went 29:53 on that climb. Read that number again and then tell me eighteen seconds is not worth a pot of wax.

The number that ends it

The friction figure is the headline. The wear figure is the one that actually pays your mortgage.

5 to 10×

Lower drivetrain wear on wax versus oil

Cassettes and chainrings, measured across Zero Friction Cycling's contamination testing, 2023 to 2025.

An oiled chain is a fly trap. Oil sits wet on the surface, collects the grit thrown up by every wet road, and then that grit gets dragged through the rollers a hundred times a minute. You are lapping your own transmission with a fine abrasive paste. Wax does not do this, because wax is dry. Grit lands on it and falls off, or sits inert on a hard surface instead of suspending itself in a lubricant.

The performance consequence shows up fastest in the wet. Friction Facts, reported through VeloNews, found waxed chains running close to six watts faster than oiled ones after just one hour in contaminated conditions. Not on day one in a clean lab. After one hour of the exact riding most of us do all winter.

Oil is fastest in the shop. Wax is fastest on the road, which is the only place that counts.

Now the running cost. A dip uses 30 to 50 pence of wax. A drip top-up is 60 pence to a pound. Amortise the kit over a couple of seasons and you are adding something like 70 pence a session. Call it one to two pounds per service, every 300 to 500 kilometres.

Against that, put the drivetrain you are no longer destroying. If you are running a premium groupset and covering serious distance, the difference between replacing cassettes and chainrings at the oiled rate and the waxed rate runs into four figures over ten thousand kilometres. The wax pays for itself long before it makes you faster, which is a strange thing to say about a performance product.

The part nobody mentions: you stop maintaining your bike

I said I have not cleaned a chain in ten years, and I meant it literally. There is no degreaser in my garage. There is no rag going black. There is no fortnightly ritual of scrubbing a cassette with a toothbrush and swearing.

The waxed routine is this. Wipe the chain. Drop it in the pot. Take it out. That is the whole job, and it happens every 300 to 500 kilometres rather than every wet ride. Modern chip products handle the factory grease so you are not standing over a tray of solvent to get started either.

For a rider with eight hours a week, this is not a small thing. Every hour you are not maintaining a bike is an hour you can ride it, or an hour you can give back to the people who tolerate your riding. Marginal gains are usually sold as speed. This one is mostly time, and time is the thing you actually lack.

Where I have proved it

Manchester to London, 2016. Complete washout. Six hours of it alone, in the kind of rain that finds its way into your shoes within twenty minutes. Waxed chain, and it was still running clean at the end of a day that would have turned an oiled chain into grinding paste.

Manchester to London, 2017. Course record, 11:43:10. That was the year I was also working on the broadcast of Sir Bradley Wiggins' Hour Record, which is a useful reminder of where all of this thinking comes from. Nobody at that level leaves three watts on the table because cleaning a chain is a habit.

Manchester to London, 2022. My fastest time on the route, on the full system.

And Sa Calobra, 29:53, on a waxed chain. Seven seconds inside the target that had beaten me twice. I am not going to claim the wax bought me those seven seconds. I am going to point out that I would not have had them to spend if I had given eighteen away at the bottom.

The honest caveats

You should know two things about me before you take any of this as gospel.

First, I spent a decade inside professional cycling as a creative director at Sky Sports and Team Sky. My instincts on marginal gains were formed in a room where this stuff was religion, and that is a bias as much as an education.

Second, I am a SILCA ambassador. The products I have named are the ones I use, and I would tell you if they had disappointed me, but you are entitled to weigh the disclosure. Every number above comes from independent laboratories, not from a brand's marketing deck, and I have linked the sources by name so you can go and argue with them rather than with me.

The genuine argument against waxing is not the performance. It is the setup cost and the batching. If you own one bike, ride two hours a week, and the chain lives on a turbo in a spare room, oil is fine and you should spend the £189 on something that will make more difference to you than eighteen seconds up a climb you are not riding. Buy the fan.

Marginal gains are not about the wheels. They are about refusing to hand back the free things.

The actual doctrine

Here is what a decade around this taught me, and it is not about chains.

The wax is not the point. The wax is what happens when you decide, once, to stop accepting the default. The default is oil because your first bike shop used oil. The default is 100psi because that is what the sidewall says. The default is training on feel because that is what everyone at the club does. None of those defaults were chosen for you by someone who tested them.

The riders who get faster are not the ones with the deepest wheels. They are the ones who audit the defaults, one at a time, and keep whatever survives contact with the evidence. Three watts here. Ten watts in your tyres. An hour of sleep. Forty grams of carbohydrate before you feel like you need them.

Compound improvements. Invisible in isolation. Significant over time.
