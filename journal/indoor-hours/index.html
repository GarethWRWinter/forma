524 hours indoors.

Five hundred and twenty four hours in a spare room, staring at a screen. It is the least romantic training I have ever done and comfortably the most useful, because the indoor hour is the only hour a working rider can actually protect. Here is the system that makes it count.

Gareth Winter

Nobody dreams about the turbo. We dream about the Col du Tourmalet in good weather with fresh legs and no need to be anywhere afterwards. Then Tuesday arrives, it is dark at half past four, someone small needs collecting, and the only training available is sixty minutes in a room that smells increasingly of you.

That hour is the whole game for a time-poor rider. It is weatherproof, it has no traffic lights, and every watt in it is deliberate. An hour indoors is worth close to ninety outside in training terms, simply because none of it is spent coasting or waiting at junctions.

So the question is not whether to do it. It is how to make the hour survive contact with your own reluctance. Five hundred and twenty four hours in, this is what I have.

1. Win the session before you clip in

Almost every abandoned turbo session was lost in the ten minutes before it started, not in the interval that broke you.

You get thirsty and the bottle is downstairs. You overheat because the fan is pointing at your left knee. The towel is in the wash so sweat is running into your eyes. Each one is a small, legitimate reason to stop, and each one arrives at exactly the moment your legs were looking for an excuse.

So the setup is not admin, it is part of the session. Bottles filled. Power meter calibrated. Towel on the bars. Fan positioned and pointed at your chest and face, not your legs. Headphones on. Workout loaded before you get on, so the first decision of the session is not what to do.

The other half of this is structure. Free riding indoors is how you end up doing forty five minutes of aimless tempo, which is the least productive intensity in cycling: too hard to recover from, too easy to drive adaptation. Load a session with a shape and let it make the decisions.

Do not stop when you are tired. Stop when you are done.

2. Fuel it like a session, not like a chore

The single most common mistake I see in amateur riders is treating an indoor hour as somehow not requiring food. It is a short ride, it is in the house, so it does not count.

It counts. A hard hour indoors is a hard hour.

Before anything with intensity in it, I want at least 40 grams of carbohydrate on board per hour of work. That can be a bar built for the job, or it can be sourdough toast with nut butter and a banana. It does not need to be exotic. It needs to be there.

Afterwards, get nutrients in within about thirty minutes. My default is poached eggs and avocado on sourdough, which lands around 28 grams of protein and 69 grams of carbohydrate, or a recovery shake at roughly three parts carbohydrate to one part protein if I have to be somewhere.

Here is the reframe that matters, and it applies to far more than the turbo. The meal after the session is not a reward for the session. It is the part of the session where the adaptation actually gets built. The ride writes the cheque. The food is where it gets cashed. Riders who skip it are paying for the training and then declining to collect it.

3. Wear the good bibs. Yes, indoors.

This one sounds like a joke and it costs people entire training blocks.

Outdoors, you move. You stand on climbs, you shift about, you freewheel down the other side. On a turbo, you are welded to one position for the entire hour, which concentrates pressure on precisely one patch of tissue. Add a worn-out chamois, which has lost the density that made it work, and you have engineered a problem.

Wear your best bibs on the turbo, not your oldest. Choose something light and genuinely moisture-wicking rather than a winter pair you are trying to use up. If the session is over two hours, consider a fresh pair at halfway, which is a faff and is much less of a faff than the alternative.

4. Take the hygiene seriously, because saddle sores end blocks

Indoor sweat is not outdoor sweat. It does not evaporate in the wind, it soaks in and stays, and it is salty. Salt plus pressure plus friction is, functionally, a cutting compound applied to the one part of you that cannot afford it.

Nobody loses their season to a dramatic crash as often as they lose three weeks to a saddle sore that they trained through out of stubbornness. The prevention is dull and it works: a chamois cream that reduces friction and carries an antibacterial, a bike fit that is not overloading the wrong tissue in the first place, and getting out of wet kit the moment you unclip rather than making coffee in it.

A ruined chamois is fifteen minutes of laundry. A ruined block is a month.

5. Learn what your head does at minute forty

The physical part of indoor training is the easy part. The hard part is that there is nowhere to go and nothing to look at, so you are alone with the commentary in your skull for the whole hour.

That commentary has a script, and it is remarkably consistent. It arrives at a predictable moment, usually the third interval, and it is very reasonable. You have had a long day. You have done enough. You will do the full session on Thursday when you are fresher.

The single most useful thing I did was to write mine down. Verbatim, excuses and all. It turns out that an argument that sounds like wisdom at minute forty looks like nonsense in daylight on a piece of paper. Once you have seen your own script, you recognise it when it starts, and recognising it is most of beating it.

The other half is knowing what you are actually agreeing to. Which brings me to the honest arithmetic of a hard session, and it is much smaller than it feels.

Minutes in a threshold session that are genuinely uncomfortable

Of those, perhaps two to four actually hurt. The rest is warm-up, recovery valleys, and time.

Quitting is a skill like any other, in the sense that it improves with practice. Every time you stop early, you make stopping early slightly easier next time. The reverse is also true, and that is the whole reason to finish sessions that do not matter much on their own.

Set something measurable to aim at: a percentage on your FTP by spring, a duration you can hold that you cannot hold today. Vague intentions do not survive minute forty. Numbers do.

What 524 hours actually taught me

That consistency is not a personality trait. It is an engineering problem.

Every single one of the five items above is a way of removing a reason to fail before it can be used. Bottle filled, so you cannot get off for a drink. Fan aimed properly, so you do not overheat. Good chamois, so nothing hurts that should not. Food ready, so the session is fuelled. Script known, so the excuse is recognisable when it arrives.

None of that is discipline. It is design. And design is available to everyone, including the tired, the busy, and the ones who do not particularly want to ride tonight, which over a winter is all of us.

Design beats discipline. Discipline runs out at minute forty; the setup you did at half past six does not.
