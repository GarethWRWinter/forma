The head 22 Sept 2023 9 min read

Three attempts at Sa Calobra. Two failures. Seven seconds.

The first attempt ended at six kilometres with my seatpost pointing the wrong way. The second was 30:47 and it broke something in me. The third was 29:53, and none of the difference came from being fitter.

Gareth Winter

Sa Calobra is 9.44 kilometres long, climbs 659 metres, and averages seven per cent. It is the most photographed road in Mallorca and it does not care about you at all. Tom Pidcock has been up it in 22:46, which is a useful number to hold in your head when you are congratulating yourself.

I wanted thirty minutes. Not because thirty is a physiologically meaningful boundary, but because it is a round number that a rider with a job and a kid can hold onto through a winter. Goals are not always rational. They only have to pull.

Monday: the DNF

Six kilometres in, going well, and my seatpost twisted to the right.

Torqued to six newton metres. Carbon grip paste applied. Both of those things done properly, by me, the night before. It moved anyway, and once your saddle is pointing at the Mediterranean rather than the road, you are done. I turned around.

The temptation on a day like that is to write it off as bad luck and file nothing. Mechanicals feel like weather: they happen to you, and there is no lesson. That is almost right, and the small part that is wrong turns out to matter. The seatpost did not fail because I was unlucky. It failed because I had never tested that setup under thirty minutes of out-of-the-saddle load on a seven per cent gradient. I had tested it in my kitchen with a torque wrench.

You have not tested a thing until you have tested it in the conditions you intend to use it.

Tuesday: 30:47, and the forty-seven seconds that would not go away

The next attempt was the one that hurt, because it was not a failure of luck. It was a failure of execution, and those come with your name on them.

Thirty minutes and forty-seven seconds. Forty-seven seconds off. The target power was 340 watts. I averaged 325.

30:47

Second attempt · 325W average against a 340W target

Forty-seven seconds outside the goal. Enough to feel like a fitness problem. It was not a fitness problem.

Here is what I did that evening, and it is the single most useful habit in this whole story. I did not go for a consoling beer and decide I needed a bigger engine. I opened the file.

The power trace told a story I had not felt while riding it. It was not a smooth 325 watts. It was a saw: a series of small collapses, dozens of them, every one of them a gear change. Sa Calobra has 26 bends and the gradient breathes constantly, and I had been chasing it, shifting to hold a comfortable cadence through every ramp and every easing.

Each shift cost me a fraction of a second of load. Individually invisible. Across thirty minutes of climbing, they compounded into most of my deficit.

That is the thing about a plateau. It almost never announces itself as one big fault you can see. It arrives as a hundred small leaks that only become visible when you stop feeling the ride and start reading it.

What actually changed

Between Tuesday and Friday I needed fifty-four seconds. I did not get fitter in three days. Nobody does. Here is where the time came from.

One: I stopped chasing cadence

I am a 95rpm rider by instinct and I had been defending that number as though it were a virtue. On a climb that changes pitch every two hundred metres, defending a cadence means shifting constantly, and shifting constantly means unloading the pedals constantly.

So I gave it up. On the steeper sections I let the cadence fall to 75 or 85 and pushed a bigger gear rather than reaching for a smaller one. Across the climb I let it range anywhere from 75 to 105 and simply stopped intervening. Fewer shifts, fewer dropouts, smoother power.

The gearing made that possible: 52x36 up front, 12x29 at the back. Enough range to survive the steep bits without needing a shift for every change in the road's mood.

Two: I made the pacing external

The problem with pacing a thirty-minute climb on feel is that feel is a liar in the first five minutes and a coward in the last five. I used live segment pacing so I could see, in real time, where I stood against the target rather than against my sensations.

At the six kilometre mark, the same point where the seatpost had ended everything on Monday, I was sixty seconds up. That number did two jobs. It told me the plan was working, and it gave me permission to spend everything that was left.

Three: I paid attention to the details that were free

The bike was 7kg. The helmet was 245 grams. The glasses were 23 grams. An extra caffeine gel, 75mg, timed for the decisive phase rather than swallowed at the bottom out of habit.

None of those things won me fifty-four seconds. All of them together bought me a handful, and when you finish seven seconds inside a target, a handful is the whole thing.

29:53

Third attempt · 400W over the final section

Seven seconds inside the target. Started at 3.30am on almost no sleep, which is not a recommendation.

The part I am not proud of

I started that ride at half past three in the morning, having barely slept, because it was the only window I had.

Every recovery principle I would coach anyone else on says do not do that. Sleep is the foundation of the whole building. Riding a maximal effort on a wrecked night is a good way to produce a disappointing number and a worse week afterwards.

I did it anyway, and it worked, and I want to be careful about the lesson because "ignore the rules and send it" is a terrible lesson. The honest version is narrower: on the day that actually counts, an accumulated season of doing it properly buys you one permission slip. You can spend that. What you cannot do is live there. Riders who make a habit of overriding their own bodies do not get faster, they get injured, ill, or bored.

One night. Not a philosophy.

What the two failures were actually for

Here is the reframe that changed how I ride, and it is the reason this piece exists rather than a straightforward brag about a time.

The DNF taught me to test things in the conditions I intended to use them. The 30:47 taught me that my deficit was in my shifting, not my legs. Neither lesson was available from the successful attempt. The 29:53 taught me nothing at all, because success teaches you almost nothing: it just confirms that whatever you did last was good enough.

The failures were the coaching. The success was just the receipt.

Most amateur riders, and I include the version of me who existed before Team Sky, treat a bad day as a verdict on themselves. The number was disappointing, so I am disappointing, and the response is to try harder next time. Trying harder is what I did on Tuesday. It produced 325 watts and a saw-toothed power file.

The professional response is duller and much more effective. A bad result is not a verdict, it is a dataset. You open the file, you find the mechanism, you change the mechanism, and you go again. The target holds. The route to it changes.

I have watched world tour teams do this at enormous expense with a room full of people. There is nothing in the method that requires the room full of people. It requires that somebody looks at the data with an honest eye, knows what to look for, and tells you the truth about what they find.

That is a coach. Most of us do not have one.

Why this is here

Forma is the coach that reads the file for you. It debriefs your ride against the conditions instead of calling a headwind a bad day, remembers what you learned last time, and holds your goal steady while the plan bends around your life. One hundred founding riders, then the doors close.
