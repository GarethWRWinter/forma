Marginal gains 15 May 2022 7 min read

The calculator was wrong about me by 225 calories a day.

I spent a day being measured properly: body scanned on a smart bike, breathing into a mask, resting metabolism read rather than estimated. Three things came back that a decade of sensible assumptions had missed entirely. One of them I had been getting wrong every single day.

Gareth Winter

Every serious amateur cyclist is running on estimates. Your training zones came from a formula involving your age. Your daily calories came from an online calculator that knows your height, your weight, and nothing else about you. Your position on the bike came from a shop fit, or from copying a photograph of a professional whose femurs are not your femurs.

None of that is stupid. Estimates are how you start. The problem is that most of us never stop, and an estimate that is 11 per cent out compounds quietly for years.

So I went to a physiology studio and had the assumptions tested. Vires Velo, with the sports scientist Hayden Daniells. Here is what came back.

1. My resting metabolism was 11 per cent higher than the internet thought

This is the finding that has changed my riding the most, and it is the least glamorous thing on the list.

Resting metabolic rate is what your body burns doing nothing at all: keeping you alive, warm, and thinking. Every calorie target you have ever set is built on top of that number, and almost nobody has actually measured theirs. They have used a calculator, which applies a population average to a specific human being.

The calculator said I burned about 2,000 calories a day at rest. The measurement said 2,225.

+225 kcal

Measured resting metabolic rate versus the calculator estimate

2,225 against an estimated 2,000. An 11 per cent shortfall, applied to every day for years, before a single pedal stroke is accounted for.

Two hundred and twenty five calories a day does not sound dramatic. Sit with what it means for a rider trying to train.

It means that on the days I thought I was eating maintenance, I was in a deficit. It means that on the days I was deliberately running a small deficit to manage weight, the deficit was substantially larger than I intended. It means that every one of those days was quietly borrowing from recovery, and it had been doing so for years.

Under-fuelling is the most common error I see in amateur cyclists and it is almost never diagnosed as under-fuelling. It presents as something else entirely. Poor durability in the last hour. Sessions that feel harder than the numbers say they should. A cold you cannot shake in February. Motivation that has quietly gone flat. Every one of those gets blamed on fitness, or age, or work stress, and gets treated with more training.

The most expensive assumption in amateur cycling is that you know how much you need to eat.

The test also came back with a macro breakdown, which turned a vague intention into a target. There is a large practical difference between "eat enough" and a number you can actually check yourself against on a Wednesday.

2. My knee problem was not in my knee

The fit used a 3D body scanning system on a smart bike, which measures what you actually do while pedalling rather than what you look like standing still in a shop.

Two things showed up. My right shoulder was sitting higher than my left, and my knees were tracking inwards through the stroke.

Both of those are things I would have described, if you had asked me before, as simply how I ride. Asymmetry feels like character. And the fix for inward knee tracking, if you go looking on the internet, is usually presented as a mobility problem or a strength imbalance, which means months of remedial work.

It was neither. It was my pedal spindles. The standard length on the pedals I was running was holding my feet too close to the cranks for my hips, and my knees were collapsing inward to accommodate the geometry. Longer spindles moved my feet out, and the tracking straightened.

The rest of the changes were tiny to the point of comedy. The saddle came down by one millimetre. The bars came closer by a few millimetres. Medium arch insoles went into the shoes.

One millimetre. If a bike fitter had told me over the phone that my saddle was a millimetre too high, I would have laughed. Measured over a hundred thousand pedal revolutions, a millimetre is not nothing, and it is certainly not nothing when it is stacked with three other small corrections that all point the same way.

3. My zones were guesses, and now they are not

The VO2 max test is the one everyone imagines when they think of a physiology lab: a mask, a ramp protocol where the power goes up every minute, and a sports scientist watching you slowly discover your limit.

The headline number is the part people talk about at the cafe. The useful part is what comes with it: training zones derived from my actual physiology rather than from a percentage of a field test, or worse, from a formula involving how old I am.

This matters most at the easy end, which is the opposite of what people expect. If your endurance zone is set slightly too high, and almost everyone's is, then every long ride you do is a bit too hard. Not hard enough to be a quality session. Just hard enough to cost you recovery and blunt the following day's intervals. Years of that produces a rider who trains constantly and improves slowly, which describes a very large number of committed amateurs.

What the day actually taught me

Not one of the three findings was about being unfit. Every one was about being wrong.

Wrong about my metabolism by 11 per cent. Wrong about the cause of a knee issue, because I had diagnosed it as a body problem when it was an equipment problem. Wrong about the intensity of my easy rides, in the direction that costs you the most and shows up the least.

That is the argument for measurement in a sentence. Not that testing makes you fitter, because it does not. Testing tells you which of your beliefs are load-bearing and which are just old.

The uncomfortable part is the cost. A day in a physiology studio is not something most riders will do annually, and I am not going to pretend it is a routine purchase. Which raises the honest question underneath this whole piece: what do you do if you cannot buy the lab?

You do the cheap version of the same discipline. You stop trusting the estimate that has never been checked. You look for the finding rather than the reassurance. When something is not working, you interrogate the mechanism instead of applying more effort to the same broken assumption. And you use your own data, which you already have far more of than my grandfather ever did, as evidence rather than decoration.

The professionals do not have better bodies than us. They have better information about the bodies they have, and somebody whose job it is to notice when the information changes.

Testing does not make you faster. It tells you which of your beliefs are actually true, which is where the speed was hiding.
