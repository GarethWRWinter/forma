Marginal gains 11 Mar 2026 8 min read

A decade defending the inner tube, and why I went tubeless anyway.

I spent ten years telling anyone who would listen that a good clincher and a latex tube beat tubeless on any road worth riding. I was right. I am now running tubeless. Both of those things can be true, and the reason why is the most useful idea in equipment choice.

Gareth Winter

Tyre arguments are the closest thing cycling has to religion, which is a shame, because tyres are one of the few areas where the evidence is unusually clear. The problem is not that people ignore the evidence. It is that they collect the evidence for one context and then apply it to every road they ever ride.

So let me make the case for both sides properly, including the case against the choice I have made, and then give you the thing I actually believe.

Start with the weight, because it is the easy part

OptionWeightNotes
Butyl inner tube90 to 120gThe default. Also the slowest.
Latex inner tube~60gFast, porous, needs pumping daily
TPU inner tube~45gLatex-level rolling resistance, packs tiny
Tubeless sealant30 to 50ml per tyreThe weight you cannot remove

A TPU tube against a tubeless setup saves you something in the order of thirty grams of rotating mass across two wheels once the sealant is accounted for. That is a real difference on a climb and an almost irrelevant one on the flat, which is the first hint that the answer is going to be conditional.

On rolling resistance, Zero Friction Cycling's testing is unambiguous about the bottom of the table: butyl is the slow one. If you are still running standard butyl tubes in 2026 because they came with the bike, that is the upgrade to make before you think about anything else on this page. TPU comes in at parity with latex, which is to say it is genuinely fast, without latex's habit of needing a pump every single morning.

The upgrade almost nobody makes is the cheapest one: stop riding butyl.

The case I made for a decade

My argument was never nostalgia. It was that the fastest riders in the world, in the events where speed is measured to the hundredth, were doing exactly what I was doing.

Tony Martin won the 2011 World Championship time trial on clinchers with latex. He won a Tour time trial stage in 2014 on an open tubular clincher with latex. He rode the 2016 Worlds on 24mm front and 26mm rear of the same construction. Julian Alaphilippe took a Tour road stage in 2020 on the same tyre. Kasper Asgreen won the 2021 Tour of Flanders on a 28mm version of it, over pavé, with a latex tube inside.

Specialized's own testing put the clincher-and-latex combination around ten watts up on the tubular setups it replaced. Remco Evenepoel was still winning world time trial titles on that construction into 2024.

That is not a fringe position. That is most of a decade of the sharpest end of the sport choosing a tube.

And then, in late 2025, Evenepoel switched to tubeless. Which is a good moment to notice that the pros were never making a philosophical choice. They were making a contextual one, over and over, and the context changed.

What actually changed my mind

Not a watts chart. My rims.

For years I rode 25mm tyres on rims with a 19 to 21mm internal width. Tubeless on that setup is a compromise at best: the tyre is not wide enough to run the low pressures that make tubeless quick, and the interface is fussier than it should be. In that context, my decade of arguing was correct.

I now ride a 28mm rim with a 25mm internal width, with a 28mm tubeless tyre that measures 30mm once it is inflated. Everything about that changes the maths. The wide internal width lets me run genuinely low pressures without the tyre squirming, and low pressure on an imperfect surface is where tubeless stops being a convenience feature and starts being free speed.

Because here is the thing about rolling resistance that the smooth-drum laboratory tests underrate: on a rough road, most of your losses are not the tyre deforming. They are you deforming. Vibration through the frame and into your body costs energy, and a supple tyre at a lower pressure absorbs the road instead of transmitting it into your arms. On a British B road in February, that is worth more than thirty grams of rotating mass.

I did not change my mind about tubes. The road under the tyres changed, and the honest answer changed with it.

The morning it all went wrong in Amsterdam

Before I sound too sure of myself, here is the failure that taught me the most about TPU, and it was entirely my fault.

Early morning, canal path, flat tyre. I had an electric pump and an early-generation TPU tube with a plastic valve. I connected the pump directly to the valve, no hose. Electric pumps generate heat. Plastic valves do not enjoy heat.

The valve deformed. Then it melted, fused into the rim hole, and became a permanent feature of the wheel. I could not fit the replacement tube because the hole was no longer a hole. I walked back to the flat carrying a bike, in cycling shoes, which is one of those experiences that makes you thoughtful about equipment.

The lesson is not that TPU is bad. Modern TPU tubes have mechanical valve attachments and welded joints precisely because the early generation had this failure mode. The lesson is that every material has a way it likes to fail, and you should find out what it is before you are standing beside a canal at six in the morning.

So: which one

Neither. Both. Chosen by the road, not by the argument you had on the internet last winter.

Reach for TPU and a clincher when

  • The road surface is good and predictable
  • The day is defined by climbing, where rotating mass is felt
  • You are chasing a time up a known climb, on a known surface
  • You want a spare that weighs 45 grams and lives in a jersey pocket without a thought

Reach for tubeless when

  • The surface is rough, patched, or genuinely British
  • The ride is long and crosses varied terrain
  • It is wet, when debris comes up off the road and punctures multiply
  • You are far from home and a puncture is a logistical problem, not just a delay

Note that the tubeless list is the description of almost every ride most of us actually do, and the TPU list is the description of the rides we picture when we buy things. That is worth sitting with.

The idea underneath the tyres

What I got wrong for a decade was not the physics. I had the physics right. What I got wrong was treating a conclusion as permanent when every input to it was temporary: my rims, my roads, the state of the products, the state of the testing.

This is the most common failure I see in riders who care about their equipment, and it is a mindset failure rather than a knowledge one. We do the research once, arrive at a position, and then spend years defending the position instead of re-running the question. The defending feels like expertise. It is the opposite.

Marginal gains are not a fixed list of correct answers you buy once. They are a habit of asking, this ride, on this road, in this weather: what is actually true today?

Most riders cannot hold that many variables in their head, on a Tuesday, in the rain, while also having a job. That is not a character flaw. It is the reason the professionals employ people to do it for them.
