The head 19 Aug 2021 6 min read

Only ten minutes of it actually hurt.

Seventy five minutes on the clock. Forty of them at threshold. Somewhere around the second interval I worked out that almost none of the session was the problem, and that I had been quitting things I was perfectly capable of finishing.

Gareth Winter

The session was four blocks of ten minutes at threshold, inside a seventy five minute ride. On paper that is unremarkable. In the spare room, at the point where you clip in and see the shape of it on screen, it looks like a wall.

That is the moment most sessions are lost. Not in the fourth interval. In the first thirty seconds, when your brain does the arithmetic on the whole thing and concludes, quite reasonably, that this is going to be horrible for over an hour.

It is not. And the gap between what it actually is and what your head has priced in is where most amateur riders are leaving fitness on the table.

Do the honest audit

Take the seventy five minutes apart.

The warm-up is not hard. It is not meant to be. Call it fifteen minutes of pleasant.

The recovery valleys between the blocks are not hard either. They are actively nice, in the specific way that only exists thirty seconds after you have stopped doing something difficult.

The cool-down is not hard. The first four or five minutes of each threshold block are not hard, because at threshold you can hold the power comfortably at first. That is what threshold means.

So what is left?

10 minutes

The genuinely uncomfortable portion of a 75-minute session

Roughly the last two and a half minutes of each of four blocks. Of those ten, perhaps two to four are what an honest person would call pain.

Ten minutes out of seventy five. Thirteen per cent of the session. And of that, the part that genuinely hurts, the part your whole body is arguing about, is two to four minutes across an hour and a quarter.

Everything else is time and cadence.

You are not quitting because it hurts. You are quitting because you are carrying all seventy five minutes at once.

The technique, which is almost embarrassingly simple

Stop looking at the session. Look at the block.

When I am in interval two, interval four does not exist. It is not on the horizon, I am not saving anything for it, I am not doing sums about whether I will still be alive when it arrives. There is this ten minutes. There is the number I am holding. That is the entire world until the timer says otherwise.

Then, inside the block, the same trick again at a smaller scale. The first half is free. The third quarter is work. The last ninety seconds are the actual event, and ninety seconds is a length of time you can be certain about. Nobody has ever been genuinely defeated by ninety seconds of anything.

The same applies on a climb. Do not stand at the bottom of Sa Calobra and try to imagine the whole nine and a half kilometres, because your brain will render it as one continuous experience of suffering and quietly begin negotiating. Ride to the next bend. There are twenty six of them, and each one is achievable in a way the climb as a whole never is.

Why this works, and it is not positive thinking

I want to be precise here, because there is a lot of nonsense written about mental toughness and most of it is decoration.

Segmenting a session works for a mechanical reason. The feeling that makes riders stop is not pain. It is the projection of pain across a duration you have not yet experienced. Your legs report the present. Your head reports an imagined future, and the imagined future is always worse than the actual one, because imagination has no data in it.

When you shrink the frame to ten minutes, or to ninety seconds, you starve the projection of the thing it needs. You cannot dread an hour you are not thinking about.

This is also why the arithmetic above is worth actually doing rather than just reading. Once you have counted the genuinely hard minutes in your own sessions, the number lives in your head as a fact rather than a reassurance. Ten out of seventy five is not a motivational poster. It is a measurement, and measurements survive minute forty in a way that encouragement does not.

The sentence in the last thirty seconds

Here is the part I only understood later, after a decade around people who do this for a living.

At the point of maximum discomfort, your head produces a sentence. It is remarkably specific and it barely changes from session to session. Mine has been broadly the same for fifteen years. Yours will be too, and I would bet money you have never actually listened to it, because when it arrives you are far too busy negotiating with it.

Next hard session, do this. In the last thirty seconds of each block, notice the exact words. Do not argue with them, do not try to feel differently, just catch the sentence.

Afterwards, write it down.

Two things happen. The first is that it usually looks pathetic in daylight, in a way it absolutely did not at the time. The second, and this is the useful one, is that once you know the sentence, you can decide in advance what you want to say back. Not a slogan. Something instructional and small: settle the shoulders, hold the number, thirty seconds.

Instructional self-talk beats motivational self-talk under load, every time. "Come on" is noise. "Smooth circles, thirty seconds left" is a job.

The commentary in your head is old material. It was written by a younger, less fit rider, and nobody has updated it since.

The wider point

The reason this matters beyond the turbo is that most riders never find out what they were actually capable of, because they stopped negotiating with a version of the session that did not exist.

A twelve-week block looks impossible in week one. It contains, realistically, about thirty genuinely difficult sessions, each with ten hard minutes in it. Five hours of discomfort spread across three months, wrapped in a great deal of pleasant riding, coffee, and daylight.

Nobody can face twelve weeks. Everyone can face Tuesday.
